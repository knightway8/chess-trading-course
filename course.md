# 2. Your route through the course

A capture removes an enemy unit. An exchange is a sequence in which both sides give up material.



## What you will learn

Decide whether a capture is safe; calculate a complete exchange; compare piece activity and pawn structure; choose a recapture; and inspect the endgame before simplifying.

## Study in three passes

First: read the method and lessons on pages 3-11 with a board.Second: solve pages 12-16 without the answers. Allow 2-4 minutes per position.Third: check pages 17-19, then apply the review routine on page 20.

## Read the moves

K = king, Q = queen, R = rook, B = bishop, N = knight. Pawns have no letter. Nxe5 means a knight captures on e5. dxc6 means the d-pawn captures on c6. + = check; # = checkmate; O-O = kingside castling; e8=Q = promotion to a queen. 1... starts with Black's move. [1]

# 3. The five-question check

Use this before committing to a capture, accepting a trade or automatically recapturing.



## 01  Is my king safe?

If you are in check, your move must answer it. After any candidate move, check whether moving that piece opens a line to your own king.

## 02  What is the strongest reply?

Look for the opponent's checks, captures and immediate threats. Do not assume a recapture. Identify pins, overloaded defenders and newly opened lines.

## 03  What is the final material balance?

Follow the sequence until the immediate tactics settle. Total everything won and lost. Count units only after checking which defenders can legally act.

## 04  Who likes the remaining position?

Compare king safety, piece activity, pawn structure and the endgame. Include the piece that recaptures: it may become the best piece on the board.

## 05  Is there a better alternative?

Compare taking now, maintaining the tension and improving a piece. Choose the move with the strongest concrete justification.

"I will play ___ because after ___, the resulting position gives me ___."



# 4. Count the whole transaction

Starting estimates: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. The king has no trade value. [2]



## A. A genuinely available piece | White to move

## B. A long sequence can lose material | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `6k1/5ppp/3p4/4p3/8/2B2N2/5PPP/6K1 w - - 0 1`

Consider 1.Nxe5 dxe5 2.Bxe5.White wins two pawns (2) and loses a knight (3). The net change is 2 - 3 = -1.Do not call this an equal trade because White makes the final capture. This line needs separate compensation to justify it; the pawn count alone does not.

# 5. Defenders and move order

Counting attackers is a starting point. Legal moves and forcing replies decide the result.



## A. The apparent defender is pinned | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `4k3/4n3/8/3p4/5N2/8/5PPP/4R1K1 w - - 0 1`

1.Nxd5 wins the pawn. The e7-knight appears to defend d5, but ...Nxd5 is illegal: it would expose Black's king to the e1-rook.This is an absolute pin. A piece pinned to a queen can still move legally; calculate what that move threatens.Recheck pins after each capture. They can disappear.

## B. Insert the forcing capture | White to move

Start with 1.Rxe8+! After 1...Qxe8 2.Qxd4, White has traded rooks and won the knight. A king move instead leaves the queen on d8 to the rook.By contrast, 1.Qxd4? Qxd4 2.Rxe8+ Kg7 gives a queen (9) for a knight and rook (8).Check the return check too: 2...Qe1+ 3.Kh2 is safe. The h3-pawn gives the king an escape. [3]



# 6. Equal value, unequal usefulness

A three-point piece may do much more work than another three-point piece.



## Trade your least useful piece for their strongest

A passive bishop for an entrenched knight can be attractive. Compare its square, targets and mobility. Then inspect the recapture. It might create a passed pawn: a pawn with no enemy pawn ahead on its file or either neighboring file.

## Remove a defender when the follow-up works

A trade can expose a king or make a target fall. Calculate the follow-up before exchanging. If another unit can replace the defender, the trade may achieve little.

## Keep the pieces that make your plan work

An attacking bishop, an active rook or a knight on an outpost can outperform its nominal value. An outpost is a useful square that enemy pawns cannot easily challenge. Keep the piece if its role matters more than the offered exchange.

## Measure the cost of the recapture

A trade can double enemy pawns but open a useful file, develop a bishop or bring a king toward the center. Compare all of those changes. A visible pawn weakness is only valuable if you can exploit it.

## Use space as a clue

With less room, exchanges can ease congestion. With more room, keeping pieces can maintain the squeeze. These are tendencies; a concrete tactical gain or a favorable endgame can outweigh them.

Name your best piece and your opponent's best piece in a recent game. Which proposed exchange would change that comparison?



# 7. You do not have to take yet

Tension means opposing units can capture each other. Keeping it preserves choices.



Position (White at bottom): `rnbqkb1r/ppp2ppp/4pn2/3p4/2PP4/2N2N2/PP2PPPP/R1BQKB1R b KQkq - 3 4`

Black to move. After1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nf3Use 4...Be7 as a normal developing move. White can then compare 5.cxd5 with 5.Bg5.This is a choice of plans. Neither a capture nor a quiet move is automatically correct.

## Taking now: 5.cxd5 exd5

A half-open file has no friendly pawns and at least one enemy pawn. After the exchange, the c-file is half-open for White and the e-file for Black. Black's c8-bishop gains a cleared diagonal through d7 and e6. White should have a plan for the new structure.

## Keeping the tension: 5.Bg5

White develops a piece and retains the c4-pawn's pressure on d5. The decision to exchange can come later. Continue checking whether Black can capture, advance or use a tactic against the center.

Take when you win something concrete, improve the resulting structure, prevent a favorable enemy break or reach an ending you want.



# 8. Choose the unit that retakes

"Capture toward the center" is a useful starting idea, never a substitute for comparing the board.



Position (White at bottom): `r1bqkbnr/1ppp1ppp/p1B5/4p3/4P3/5N2/PPPP1PPP/RNBQK2R b KQkq - 0 4`

Black to move. Ruy Lopez Exchange Variation:1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Bxc64...dxc6: opens the d-file and the c8-bishop's diagonal.4...bxc6: opens the b-file and retains the d-pawn, which can influence the center.Both recaptures leave doubled c-pawns. Their other effects differ.

## Compare four consequences

King: do I open my own shelter?Activity: which piece or file becomes available?Structure: do I create isolated, doubled or passed pawns?Tactics: is an intermediate move stronger than either recapture?

## A tempting follow-up is not a free pawn

After 4...dxc6, try 5.Nxe5? Qd4. Black attacks the knight and the e4-pawn. In 6.Nf3 Qxe4+, Black recovers the pawn with check. This illustrates why removing a defender does not finish the calculation.

Compare every legal recapture. Include a check, threat or counter-capture if it changes the result.



# 9. Preview the endgame first

Remove the queens in your mind, place the recapturing piece correctly, and evaluate that exact position.



## A. A clear plan after the queens go | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `2kq4/8/3QK3/4P3/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`

1.Qxd8+ Kxd8 2.Kf7 reaches a winning king-and-pawn ending. White's king controls the route ahead of the pawn.One illustrative continuation is 2...Kd7 3.e6+ Kd6 4.e7 Kd7 5.e8=Q+.Black has other king moves; the principle is to keep the king supporting the pawn's advance.

## B. An extra rook-pawn may be insufficient | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `kq6/8/PK1Q4/8/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`

White is in check. 1.Qxb8+ Kxb8 reaches a drawn rook-pawn ending: Black can occupy a8.For example, 2.a7+ Ka8 3.Ka6 is stalemate. White cannot force the king out of the corner with only this pawn and king.This evaluates the resulting ending; it does not claim a forced win exists before the trade.



# 10. When ahead, when behind

Material advantage changes your priorities. It does not make every exchange helpful.



## When ahead: reduce useful enemy resources

Look for safe trades that reduce counterplay and preserve a winning plan. Keep enough material to checkmate or create a passed pawn. Trading pawns can remove your route to victory; trading an active piece can also give away control.

## When behind: look for specific drawing routes

Keeping active pieces often preserves threats and practical chances. But exchanges can be excellent if they remove all the opponent's winning material, reach a known drawn ending or eliminate a dangerous attack. "Never trade when behind" is too crude.

Position (White at bottom): `4rk2/8/8/8/2B5/8/8/4R1K1 w - - 0 1`

White to move: an extra bishop.After 1.Rxe8+ Kxe8, only king and bishop versus king remain. Neither side can checkmate by any legal sequence, so the game is drawn. [1]The initial extra bishop does not itself prove a win. The example shows why reducing the piece count is not the same as converting an advantage.

After this exchange, what is my actual winning method? If you cannot name one, inspect the ending before trading.



# 11. The result outranks the count

Two final checks: can I finish the game, and does this capture accidentally end it in a draw?



## A. A free pawn can be the wrong capture | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `7k/8/5Kp1/6Q1/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`

1.Qxg6? is stalemate. The black king is not in check and has no legal move.1.Kxg6 preserves the win. The white king now blocks the queen's g-file, so Black can play ...Kg8. White retains queen and king against a bare king.After a final capture, check the opponent's legal moves. [1]

## B. Checkmate before recapturing | White to move

Position (White at bottom): `6k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/5PPP/3bR1K1 w - - 0 1`

White can capture the bishop with 1.Rxd1, but 1.Re8# ends the game at once.A material loss does not oblige you to recapture on the next move. Look for checks and threats before choosing the natural capture."Checks first" is a search habit, not an instruction to play a bad check.

# 17. Calculate before you judge

Award one point for the move or judgment and one for the explanation. Maximum: 20 points.



## 01 / Take the bishop

1.Nxe5. Black has no immediate recapture on e5; White gains a bishop, about 3 points. The white king is safe. A later pawn move such as ...f6 is not a recapture: White gets another turn to move the knight.

## 02 / The sequence loses three points

After 1.Rxd5 cxd5 2.Qxd5, White has won two pawns (2) and lost a rook (5): 2 - 5 = -3. No material profit exists in the stated line. White would need concrete compensation to justify starting it.

## 03 / The defender is absolutely pinned

1.Nxe6 is legal. Black's ...Nxe6 would expose the c8-king to the c1-rook along the c-file, so that reply is illegal. Do not count the c7-knight as an available recapturing defender in this position.

## 04 / Trade rooks with check first

1.Rxe8+ Qxe8 2.Qxd5 wins the knight. A king move instead leaves the queen on d8 to the rook. The return check 2...Qe1+ 3.Kh2 is safe: the h3-pawn gives White an escape square.The tempting 1.Qxd5? Qxd5 2.Rxe8+ Kg7 costs a queen (9) for a knight and rook (8), a net loss of 1. The order matters.

Return to pages 4-5. Put each captured piece beside the board and total both piles before evaluating the position.



# 18. Explain the position left behind

Strategic answers are graded for sound consequences, not for guessing an engine's first choice.



## 05 / A trade of different advantages

After 4.Bxc6 dxc6, White has exchanged a bishop for a knight and doubled Black's c-pawns. Black keeps both bishops and opens lines for development. 4.Ba4 retains White's light-squared bishop and preserves a different structure.A complete answer names both a benefit and a cost. The e5-pawn is not simply free: 5.Nxe5? Qd4 6.Nf3 Qxe4+ recovers a pawn with check.

## 06 / Fix the structure or keep the option

5.cxd5 exd5 leaves the c-file half-open for White and the e-file half-open for Black. It also clears the c8-bishop's diagonal. 5.Bg5 develops the bishop and keeps the central pawn tension.Neither answer is justified by "I always take" or "I never take." Identify which resulting structure fits your plan.

## 07 / Preserve the pawn shelter

1.Qxf3 captures with the queen and keeps f2, g2 and h2 intact. 1.gxf3 leaves doubled f-pawns on f2 and f3 and removes the g-pawn from the king's shelter. The g-file becomes half-open for White.Given the question's no-immediate-tactic condition, Qxf3 is the straightforward structural choice. In a full game, calculate both: opening a file can sometimes support an attack.

Compare the recapturing piece, the files and the pawn structure. A trade changes more than the material count.



# 19. Check how the game ends

The most important consequence of an exchange may be the result itself.



## 08 / A dead position

1.Rxd8+ Kxd8 leaves White with a king and bishop, Black with a king. Checkmate is impossible by any legal continuation, so this is a draw. An extra piece is not enough if the remaining material cannot deliver mate. [1]

## 09 / One capture draws, one keeps the win

1.Qxb6? is stalemate. Black's king on a8 is not in check. The queen covers b8 and a7; b7 is also covered. There are no other black units to move.1.Kxb6 preserves the win. The king on b6 blocks its own queen's b-file, leaving 1...Kb8 legal. White still has king and queen against king. The exact location of the capturing unit changes the result.

## 10 / Mate on the back rank

1.Rd8#. The rook attacks g8 along the eighth rank. Black's own pawns occupy f7, g7 and h7; f8 and h8 are covered by the rook. The bishop cannot capture the rook or block the check. Capturing the bishop with Rxc1 misses an immediate finish.

# 20. A repeatable practice routine
